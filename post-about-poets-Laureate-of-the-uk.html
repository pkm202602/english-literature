★ What is a Poet Laureate?

The Poet Laureate of the United Kingdom is an honorary position appointed by the monarch, currently on the advice of the Prime Minister. The title originates from the ancient Greek and Roman tradition of crowning poets with a wreath of laurel — a symbol of achievement and honour.

Origins: The modern laureateship began in 1616, when King James I granted a pension to Ben Jonson. However, the first official Poet Laureate was John Dryden, appointed by King Charles II in 1668. The position was originally held for life and came with a salary of £200 per year, plus a butt (126 gallons) of Canary wine — a tradition revived by Ted Hughes with 720 bottles of sherry.

Duties: Historically, the Poet Laureate was expected to write verse for royal birthdays, coronations, and other national occasions. Today, the role is largely honorary, with the holder free to decide when and what to write for significant national events.

Modern Era: Since 1999, the appointment has been for a fixed ten-year term, rather than for life. Andrew Motion was the first to serve a fixed term, and Carol Ann Duffy was the first woman, first Scot, and first openly LGBTQ+ person to hold the role.
